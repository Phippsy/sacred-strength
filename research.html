Our conversations

What Heather has told us so far

Three conversations between Heather and Donal in October 2026, recorded in a noisy room, so some details may be slightly off.

Conversation 1

The idea

  • Four phases of the cycle, each with different energy and needs. Train, rest and eat in step with them.
  • Personal training and yoga together. “Making women stronger, not smaller.”
  • A reaction against an industry that keeps pushing for more: protein, calories, harder plans, “new year, new you” in midwinter.
  • Medicine and fitness advice were built around men. Women's health is under-researched (Heather mentioned Unwell Women).
  • Free cycle tracking as the way in; paid online coaching as the business. Online first, with local classes too.
  • Strength matters more as women approach perimenopause.
Conversation 2

The coaching

  • A consultation, then a weekly plan of about four sessions, chosen by the client and placed by phase.
  • Every session has an alternative. But for most days: just follow the plan.
  • Yoga filmed for a library, streamed live, and in person locally.
  • A 12-week plan sells well, but must not be a quick fix. Twelve weeks is about three cycles.
  • A possible niche: life after weight-loss jabs, when habits haven't changed.
  • PT qualified by the end of 2026, yoga around March 2027. Start with her own network.
Conversation 3

Feelings first

  • Everything Heather describes is about how women feel. An app can record that; only a person can respond to it.
  • The cycle is a way into body awareness, not the goal.
  • Being listened to may be what people are paying for. Two hours with a practitioner helped her more than ten minutes with a GP.
  • Irregular cycles, perimenopause and women who don't know their dates need a feelings-led plan and more one-to-one time.
  • Some clients want to be told, others want to choose. Ask them.

Who's out there

Plenty of apps and content. Nobody offering a real coach.

About 15 relevant products fall into four groups. There's also a growing long tail of small, often AI-built “cycle sync workout” apps with few users.

Group 1

Big cycle trackers

Huge user bases, built around period prediction, fertility and pregnancy. No workout guidance by phase.

Flo

570M+ downloads and 70M+ monthly users. London-based.

Clue

100M+ downloads. Berlin-based, positions itself on privacy and science.

Natural Cycles

A regulated, temperature-based contraceptive. Paid only.

Apple Health

Free on every iPhone. Predictions stay on the phone.

Group 2

Trackers that add lifestyle tips

Track the cycle and suggest food, movement and rest by phase, mostly as content.

Lively

“Cycle syncing” tracker with AI chat. Probably the one Heather saw.

Moody Month

UK, women-built. Daily forecasts, movement and mindfulness videos.

Hormona

At-home hormone tests and recipes. Strong perimenopause angle.

MyFLO

The original “Cycle Syncing” brand, which is trademarked.

Group 3

Workout and meal subscriptions

Closest to Heather's idea, but content rather than a coach.

28

Phase-matched videos and nutrition. Aimed at women in their early 20s.

Cloud Nine Collective

UK creator-led. 200+ videos from yoga to heavy lifting. £18 a month.

Sync and Sculpt

Built by a hormone health coach and Pilates instructor. Very small so far.

Alo Moves SYNCD

Big-brand yoga and mindfulness series by phase.

Group 4

Coaching for athletes, and wearables

Data-driven. Readiness scores and AI coaching.

Wild.AI

UK-founded “smart coach for women”. Bought by Zepp Health in 2025.

WHOOP

The only big player that changes training advice by phase. Needs the band.

Oura

Adjusts its readiness score for the cycle. No workout plans.

FitrWoman

Free tracking plus a coach portal for sports teams.

Gaps

Where Sacred Strength could fit

1

A human coach

Others offer videos, recipes or AI chat. Nobody offers affordable personal coaching built around the client's own tracked cycle and feelings.

2

A gentler voice

The big trackers are clinical or fertility-led, the performance apps are data-heavy, and some cycle-sync brands still focus on bodies.

3

Women in their 30s

Others aim at early 20s, Gen Z, athletes or the mid-40s. The 30s fall in between.

4

Strength and yoga together

Most offers are either workouts or yoga and mindfulness, rarely both in one weekly plan.

5

Light, private tracking

Mainstream apps ask dozens of questions and share data with advertisers. Asking little stands out.

6

Local and online

Online-only apps can't run a class or a walk in Leigh-on-Sea.

A caution: the lack of a combined offer could mean an opening, or that it's hard to make pay. Human coaching doesn't scale like an app.

The science

Thinner than social media suggests

The research doesn't show that training by phase gets better results on average. It does support listening to how you feel and adjusting, which is exactly Heather's message.

Women vary a lot, so notice how you feel and adjust.

2020 · 78 studies

Performance may be very slightly lower during and just after the period. The effect is trivial, the evidence low quality, and the authors recommend a personal approach. McNulty et al.

2023 · review of reviews

Too little good evidence to say phase changes strength gains. Calls phase-based prescriptions “not evidence-based” and recommends tracking symptoms alongside sleep, stress and food. Colenso-Semple et al.

2024 · 22 studies

Some strength effects, but small samples and a very small effect on everyday gym-type strength. Niering et al.

2025 · 100 TikTok videos

Most #cyclesyncing videos oversimplify the science. NYU summary

Health data and the rules

Collect little, keep it private, make no medical claims

Cycle data is sensitive, and trust in period apps has been knocked. Doing less is both easier and a selling point.

What competitors collect

Flo's sign-up runs to about 55 screens before a paywall and logs 70+ symptoms. Flo, Clue, Natural Cycles and Stardust all link sensitive data to the user's identity on their App Store labels. Apple is the exception: predictions stay on the phone.

Why women are wary

Flo settled with the US regulator in 2021 over sharing data with Facebook and Google, and a US jury found Meta liable in 2025. In 2026 Mozilla found Stardust sending health data to an analytics firm.

UK GDPR

Cycle data is health data, a “special category”. It needs explicit consent. ICO guidance. The ICO's 2023–24 review of 37 apps flagged over-collection and bundled consent. Report

Medical devices and advertising

Fitness apps and simple cycle diaries are unlikely to be medical devices; apps that help with conception or contraception may be. MHRA. Health claims in adverts need evidence. CAP Code
